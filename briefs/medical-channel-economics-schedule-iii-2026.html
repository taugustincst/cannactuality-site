Medical-Channel Economics After Schedule III: What DEA Registration Changes for Product Teams

Published November 11, 2026 · CannActuality Research Desk · For licensed vendors and product teams

Key takeaways

  • The medical pathway is live. On October 30, 2026, the Justice Department announced DEA's first registrations under the April 2026 order: more than 900 dispensaries in Alabama, Florida, Mississippi, South Dakota, Texas, Virginia and West Virginia. Manufacturer and distributor applications are still under review.
  • November 29, 2026 is the next hard date. DEA guidance expects "timely applicants" to source only from other timely applicants or registrants by then. Every medical SKU's inputs now carry a federal status question.
  • Labeling stays state-led, with one federal addition. Registrants may follow state packaging and labeling rules, plus the 21 U.S.C. § 825(c) anti-transfer warning "where applicable."
  • § 280E relief has been promised but not issued as formal guidance. Treasury and the IRS have previewed relief for medical activity, but as of November 11, 2026 we found no published notice or regulation. Model both outcomes.
  • Broader rescheduling is still undecided. The ALJ's stay ran out on its own terms when reply briefs closed on November 5, 2026. We found no recommended decision as of today.

What the order did, and what it did not do

The final order (AG Order No. 6754-2026) was signed April 22 and announced April 23. It was published and took effect on April 28, 2026 (91 Fed. Reg. 22714). It relies on 21 U.S.C. § 811(d)(1), the treaty-based authority, and amends 21 C.F.R. parts 1300, 1301, 1308 and 1312. It adds three categories to Schedule III at 21 C.F.R. § 1308.13(g):

  • marijuana in an FDA-approved product or subject to a state medical marijuana license;
  • marijuana extract under the same two conditions;
  • naturally derived delta-9 THC under the same conditions, with hemp excluded.

Several categories stay in Schedule I: unlicensed bulk material, synthetically derived THC (the order gives delta-10 THC as an example) and anything non-medical. New § 1301.13(k)(1)(iv) says registrations "do not authorize the manufacture, distribution, dispensing, or use of marijuana" for non-medical purposes. A "state medical marijuana license" is a license issued by a state, D.C. or territorial entity that authorizes manufacturing, distribution or dispensing for medical purposes.

The registration pathway: 21 C.F.R. § 1301.13(k)

ElementWhat the rule says
Registration typesManufacturer (cultivate, produce, process, package, label, transfer), distributor and dispenser. One entity may hold more than one type.

Labeling and packagingRegistrants are exempt from the federal labeling, packaging and sealing requirements of 21 C.F.R. part 1302 if they follow state law. The label must also carry the § 825(c) warning "where applicable."
Security and disposalMeeting state physical-security rules is sufficient, and disposal may follow state law.
DispensingA state medical certification is sufficient if it is dated and signed on the day issued and names the patient and address and the practitioner's state license number.
Treaty mechanicsManufacturers set a nominal crop price. DEA buys the crop at that price and resells it at the same price plus an administrative fee. Crops must be stored where DEA can inspect them, and the registration specifies cultivation areas.

Section 825(c) requires a warning on Schedule II–IV drugs dispensed to a patient that "it is a crime to transfer the drug to any person other than the patient." The order does not define "where applicable."

Implementation since October 30

DOJ calls the October 30 dispensary registrations a "first phase." Manufacturer and distributor applications still need facility inspections, production controls and quota decisions. DOJ adds that state licensure alone does not guarantee registration, and that registrants must meet federal security, recordkeeping and inventory-control requirements.

DEA's "Timely Applicant Information for Medical Marijuana Applicants" (October 30, 2026) sets the supply-chain rule. According to reporting by Cannabis Business Times and Foley Hoag:

  • Timely applicants may transact only with other timely applicants and registrants, and must check the status of their counterparties.
  • They are expected to have compliant supply chains by November 29, 2026.
  • Dispensaries and distributors may sell through inventory acquired before the guidance, even from non-qualifying sources. Foley Hoag notes that this allowance is not expressly extended to manufacturers.
  • DEA does not plan to penalize sending small quantities to unregistered state-licensed testing labs.

Foley Hoag notes that the guidance does not have the force of law. DEA has also proposed denying a Minneapolis dispensary's application on three grounds:

  • Minnesota's dual-purpose license is not a "state medical license" with robust protections against diversion;
  • veterans may self-attest to qualifying status;
  • the state allows dispensing of synthetic and artificially derived cannabinoids, which remain in Schedule I.

Two legal tracks remain open. On September 9, 2026, the D.C. Circuit denied a motion to stay the medical order. That keeps the order in force, but the merits are undecided. Harris Sliwoski expects a ruling no earlier than about March 2027.

In the broader rescheduling proceeding, Chief ALJ Derek C. Julius admitted the GAO report and denied a request to reopen the hearing on October 22. He allowed limited briefs, due October 29, with replies due November 5. His order said the stay "will expire by its own terms at the conclusion of this briefing schedule." We found no recommended decision as of November 11.

Which medical programs are most exposed

Cannabis Business Times reports DEA's timely-applicant list at nearly 7,000 applicants: 4,197 dispensaries, 2,086 manufacturers and 710 distributors. The largest state pools are shown below.

Foley Hoag describes the seven first-phase states as medical-only markets, and notes that Virginia has not yet begun adult-use licensing. The supply rule bites hardest where few upstream businesses applied. Harris Sliwoski notes Iowa appears to have only two applicants and warns that "without registered cultivators, dispensaries will lack a lawful source of marijuana." In Minnesota, 30 businesses applied, and more than 300 dispensary licensees did not.

Dual-licensed operators: the split-portfolio problem

The order does not address co-located or shared medical and adult-use operations. Foley Hoag's reading is that "adult-use activity falls entirely outside the federal registration framework," and that DEA has not said how much separation is enough. The practical questions depend on how each state licenses the two channels:

  • Single combined licenses. In states such as Minnesota, it is unclear whether an operator can register at all. Harris Sliwoski notes that a product's medical or adult-use status there is not settled until the point of sale.
  • Separate or separable licenses. California's DCC emergency rules let licensees change A/M designation at any time and created a path to split combined A/M retail licenses on the same premises. New Jersey restricts medical product from moving into adult-use retail.
  • Traceability. Foley Hoag advises that operators be able to show through inventory tracking that medical product stays in designated channels. Dickinson Wright lists as still open whether medical inventory can ever return to adult-use channels.

The § 280E question: what is and is not settled

This section is research, not legal or tax advice. Section 280E positions should be decided with qualified tax counsel.

What the official record says. The order states that state licensees "will no longer be subject to the deduction disallowance imposed by Section 280E." It also says "nothing in this rule constitutes a determination regarding federal tax liability," and it encourages Treasury to consider retrospective relief.

Treasury's April 23, 2026 release says rescheduling "generally removes" § 280E for activity that no longer involves Schedule I or II substances. It says relief generally applies for the full taxable year that includes the order's effective date. It also says guidance will clarify that, for businesses with more than one activity, § 280E applies only to the Schedule I/II activity, for example by apportioning expenses. Adult-use activity remains Schedule I.

What is not settled.

  • No formal guidance yet. The IRS's 2026–2027 Priority Guidance Plan (September 29, 2026) lists "Guidance under §280E," but we found no notice or regulation published as of November 11.
  • Whether registration is required. Holland & Hart sets out two readings: relief follows from the scheduling change alone, or unregistered handling remains federally prohibited and keeps § 280E in play.
  • How expenses are apportioned between medical and adult-use activity.
  • Prior years. In New Mexico Top Organics v. Commissioner (Tax Court Docket No. 19661-24, pending), the IRS continues to reject non-280E positions, according to Foley Hoag.
  • Litigation risk. Holland & Hart notes that if the D.C. Circuit vacates the order, § 280E "could be back."

What this means for product teams

  • Audit every medical SKU's inputs before November 29. List each input (flower, biomass, distillate, co-packer, white-label partner) and its counterparty's timely-applicant or registration status. Manufacturers should not assume they can sell through non-qualifying inventory.
  • Keep medical lines to naturally derived delta-9. Synthetic and artificially derived cannabinoids remain Schedule I, and DEA cited them in the Minnesota show-cause order.
  • Plan artwork for the § 825(c) warning. State rules still govern labels. Reserve space and a change process for the federal anti-transfer warning until DEA clarifies "where applicable."
  • Separate medical and adult-use SKUs. Use distinct SKU codes, inventory tags and, where state rules allow, distinct packaging. Do not assume product can move between channels.
  • Run two sets of numbers for § 280E. Show Outcome A and Outcome B side by side in medical-line business cases until formal IRS guidance is published.
  • Watch upstream registrations. Manufacturer and distributor registrations have not been issued yet. Medical launches depend on suppliers clearing inspection and quota review.
