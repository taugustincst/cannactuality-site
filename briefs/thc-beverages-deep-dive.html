THC Beverages: The Dose Moved, the Channel Moved, the Clock Is Ticking

Published October 29, 2026 · CannActuality Research Desk · For licensed vendors and product teams

Key takeaways

  • Convenience-store sales of hemp-derived THC beverages rose 74% year over year, and the channel now accounts for nearly a quarter of tracked THC beverage sales — second only to liquor stores (NielsenIQ data presented at the 2026 NACS Show).
  • The center of gravity on strength moved: 10 mg beverages now account for 56% of convenience-store sales by THC content, up from 16% in 2025, while 5 mg fell to 19% (NielsenIQ).
  • Seltzers lead the channel (37% of sales), ahead of sodas (31%) and mocktails (22%); single-serve sales grew 122% year over year (NielsenIQ).
  • Trial is not the problem — repeat is: buyers grew 56% year over year, but only 34% made a second purchase (NielsenIQ).
  • Every hemp-channel plan now runs against a federal deadline: most intoxicating hemp products lose federal hemp status on December 11, 2026. See the regulatory watch brief.

Two beverage markets, one development team

Cannabis beverages now develop along two distinct tracks. In licensed dispensaries, beverages remain a small but growing category: California recorded $7.43 million in beverage sales in April 2026, up 8.0% in dollars and 14.6% in units year over year, with the average price easing from $7.52 to $7.09 (Headset data). In mainstream retail, hemp-derived THC beverages have built a second market in liquor and convenience stores — and the convenience channel is where the 2026 growth story lives.

The convenience channel, by the numbers

NielsenIQ data presented at the 2026 NACS Show (Las Vegas, October 2026) shows convenience-store hemp THC beverage sales up 74% year over year, with the channel taking nearly a quarter of tracked THC beverage sales. The case study retailers cited is instructive: South Carolina chain Spinx generated more than $267,000 in THC beverage sales in its first three months after introducing the products in late 2025 — with no advertising spend — and has since passed $1.8 million across its nearly 100 stores. Spinx projects $2.3 million next year if the products remain legal.

The structural details product teams should design against:

  • Strength: 10 mg is 80% of Spinx's THC beverage sales, matching the national NielsenIQ read (56% of channel sales by THC content for 10 mg, vs 19% for 5 mg and 10% for 20 mg).
  • Style: seltzers 37%, sodas 31%, mocktails 22% of convenience-store sales.
  • Flavor: Circana's 2026 retail data puts fruit blends first at 19% of tracked dollar sales, then orange (11%), lemon (10%), and pineapple (4%) — with orange the fastest grower, up 270% year over year.
  • Pack: singles are growing more than twice as fast as four-packs (+122% vs +53% year over year), though four-packs still hold 51% of revenue to singles' 45%.
  • Decision window: presenters at NACS put the convenience shopper's attention at roughly 30 seconds — packaging and strength callouts do the selling, not education.

The repeat-purchase problem

The category's weak metric is retention. Earlier NielsenIQ research cited at the show found beverage buyers up 56% year over year, but only 34% made a second purchase, averaging about two trips and $52 in annual spending. Circana's 2026 consumer research is more encouraging — 60% of beverage buyers said they were willing to try or continue THC-infused products over the next 12 months — but willingness is not velocity. Beverage brands that treat trial as the finish line will keep re-buying their own customers. Experienced operators budget the channel as a 12-to-18-month investment, in the words of Delta Beverages' CSO at the show, with cooler placement and promotional support as real line items, not afterthoughts.

The regulatory clock on the hemp channel

The convenience and liquor-store beverage market exists because hemp-derived products sit outside state cannabis licensing. That foundation closes on a published schedule: cannabinoids the plant cannot naturally produce lose federal hemp status on November 12, 2026, and the rest of the federal redefinition — including a 0.4 mg total-THC-per-container cap that no current intoxicating beverage can meet — takes effect December 11, 2026. California has already moved intoxicating hemp into licensed channels under AB 8. NACS presenters described buyers as enthusiastic but cautious ahead of December for exactly this reason. Any beverage development plan that does not state, in writing, which side of December 11 each SKU lives on is not finished. Full analysis in our Regulatory Watch brief.

What this means for product teams

  • Anchor the line at 10 mg for mainstream retail. The 2025 assumption that low-dose (5 mg) leads in convenience is a year out of date. Keep a session-strength tier, but expect 10 mg to carry volume.
  • Lead with seltzer, differentiate on flavor. Seltzer is the default style; fruit blends and citrus — orange above all — are where flavor development is paying off.
  • Design singles first. Single-serve is the trial engine and the fastest-growing pack format in convenience; four-packs monetize the converted.
  • Budget for retention, not just placement. With only about a third of buyers repeating, spend on the second purchase — pack inserts, loyalty hooks, consistent strength — not only on getting into the cold vault.
  • Run a two-track regulatory plan. For every hemp-derived SKU, document the licensed-channel path (state cannabis licensing, testing, track-and-trace) or an exit before December 11, 2026. Dispensary-channel beverages are unaffected by the federal hemp dates and keep growing at high single digits.
