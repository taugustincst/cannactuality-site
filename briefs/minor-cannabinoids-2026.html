Minor Cannabinoids in Product Development: CBN, CBG, THCV and the 2026 Formulation Map

Published November 9, 2026 · CannActuality Research Desk · For licensed vendors and product teams

Key takeaways

  • Minor cannabinoids are a ratio business, not a standalone one. In BDSA's licensed-market tracking, gummies accounted for about 75% of sales dollars for CBN-containing products and about 70% for CBG-containing products — and 2026 point-of-sale data and launches show the same pattern: CBN, CBG and THCV appear inside multi-cannabinoid gummies, tablets and softgels alongside THC and CBD, not as single-cannabinoid products.
  • CBG is the largest minor cannabinoid by modelled US market share — about 45% in 2024 — while CBN is the fastest-growing, at a forecast 15% CAGR (Emergen Research). THCV is the scarcest of the three: most cannabis plants yield less than 0.2% THCV, according to the Washington State Liquor and Cannabis Board's July 2026 research brief.
  • How a minor cannabinoid is made is now a legal variable, not just a cost variable. Washington permits only naturally extracted THCV in its licensed market and prohibits semi-synthetic conversion. Federally, cannabinoids the plant cannot naturally produce lose hemp status on November 12, 2026; natural cannabinoids that were synthesized or manufactured outside the plant — and finished hemp products above 0.4 mg of total THC per container — follow on December 11, 2026.
  • California preserves one narrow hemp lane for CBN: under AB 8, CBD or CBN isolate above 99% purity, with no THC and no synthetic cannabinoids, may still be used in foods and beverages outside the licensed system (California Department of Public Health). Everything intoxicating in California moves through licensed channels.

The map in one view: fast growth, small base

Two kinds of evidence describe this market, and they measure different things. Emergen Research models the total US minor cannabinoid market — licensed cannabis, hemp-derived and wellness channels combined — at $1.5 billion in 2024, projected to reach $4.5 billion by 2034 at an 11.5% compound annual growth rate. That is a modelled estimate, not point-of-sale data, and its scope is deliberately broad. BDSA's Retail Sales Tracking measures the narrower licensed dispensary channel, and its most detailed public cut remains the baseline every later dataset builds on: in Q1 2022, across California, Colorado, Illinois, Massachusetts, Michigan, Nevada and Oregon, there were about 70% more products containing CBN than a year earlier, and more than twice as many products containing CBG.

The growth rates were dramatic precisely because the base was small. In the mature markets BDSA tracks separately, Q1 2022 sales of CBG-containing products rose 655% year over year in California, 1,105% in Nevada, 1,185% in Oregon and 1,251% in Colorado, while CBN-containing product sales rose 22% to 145% in the same states. In those same markets, CBD-containing product sales were still roughly three times CBN sales and more than nine times CBG sales — and CBD sales were falling by double digits. Consumer familiarity explains the headroom: BDSA consumer data put familiarity with CBN at 14% and with CBG at 13% in fall 2021, up from 11% and 8% in fall 2019. Minor cannabinoids are where formulation differentiation is happening; they are not yet where most dollars are.

Where they show up: gummies first, then tablets and softgels

The format map is unusually concentrated. In BDSA's Q1 2022 data, gummies took about 75% of sales dollars for CBN-containing products and about 70% for CBG-containing products. The next formats were an order of magnitude smaller: for CBN, chocolate and tinctures at about 5% each; for CBG, tinctures at about 10% and taffy at about 5%. A development plan that starts anywhere other than gummies is starting away from the demonstrated demand.

Current licensed-market data shows the pattern holding in 2026. Headset's October 2026 brand data for Jams — an edibles-only brand whose top 10 products account for 83.1% of its sales — lists four multi-cannabinoid ratio gummies in that top 10: a THC/CBD/CBN 2:1:1 gummy as the brand's number-one product at 17.2% of brand sales, a THC/CBG/THCV 2:1:1 gummy at 7.5%, a THC/CBD/CBG 2:1:1 gummy at 4.7%, and a CBD/CBN/THC 1:1:1 gummy at 2.4%. The ratio is printed as the product's identity; the minor cannabinoid is the differentiator inside a format buyers already understand.

The 2026 launch record adds two formats beside gummies. In October 2026, Ascend Wellness Holdings launched a seven-formula tablet line in Massachusetts, Illinois and New Jersey in which every formula pairs THC with one or more minor cannabinoids: CBN in the two nighttime-positioned formulas, THCV and CBC in the daytime-positioned formula, and CBG in three of the seven. In May 2026, Canopy Growth's Spectrum Therapeutics expanded its medical softgel lineup to 30- and 90-count packs built entirely on minor-cannabinoid ratios — CBD:CBG 1:1, CBD:CBC:CBG 2:1:1 and 5:2:2, CBD:CBN 2:1, and CBN:CBD:THC 1:1:2. Note the supporting player in both lineups: CBC, rarely headlined, appears in Ascend's THCV formula and Canopy's core daily softgel. The 2026 formulation map is therefore a ladder — gummies for reach, tablets and softgels for measured, repeatable formats — with CBN, CBG, THCV and CBC assigned by use occasion in the brand's own labelling.

A discipline note on that labelling: brand positioning is market evidence about what companies believe buyers want; it is not evidence of effect. CannActuality reports positioning as positioning. The Washington LCB's THCV brief makes the underlying point plainly — human research on THCV is limited and the bulk of the literature is preclinical — and product teams should hold the same line in their own claims review.

Cannabinoid by cannabinoid: three different supply problems

CBN (cannabinol) — the degradation product with the clearest format fit. CBN is not a primary plant product: it forms as THC oxidizes with age, heat and light, which is why commercial supply depends on controlled conversion and purification of plant-derived material rather than on a CBN-rich harvest. Its market position is the most established of the three: BDSA found the top-selling CBN products in California, Colorado and Illinois were positioned around nighttime use, and Emergen projects CBN as the fastest-growing minor cannabinoid segment at a 15% CAGR. California's AB 8 also gives CBN a distinction no other minor cannabinoid in this brief has: CBN isolate above 99% purity, with no THC, is expressly preserved as an ingredient that can be added to foods in the state's hemp program, per the Department of Public Health.

CBG (cannabigerol) — the precursor, harvested early or bred for. CBG's acid form, CBGA, is the biosynthetic precursor the plant converts into THCA, CBDA and CBCA, so by maturity most CBGA has already been converted onward and CBG itself remains at low levels in mature plants. Commercial CBG supply therefore comes mainly from CBG-dominant hemp cultivars bred for that profile. CBG is the largest minor cannabinoid segment in Emergen's US model at about 45% share in 2024, and its licensed-market products skew to daytime-positioned ratios — BDSA's top-selling CBG product in California and Colorado was a 1:1 CBG pear gummy, and Canopy's 2026 softgel expansion leads its daytime line with a CBD:CBG 1:1 formulation.

THCV (tetrahydrocannabivarin) — the scarce varin. THCV is the propyl-side-chain counterpart of THC, produced through a separate varin pathway (from CBGVA, not CBGA). The Washington LCB's July 2026 research brief — written because the state's licensing team asked whether THCV is only available synthetically — reports that most cannabis plants yield less than 0.2% THCV, that certain African-origin cultivars such as Durban Poison are reported to test higher (while noting cultivar names are not reliable indicators and the batch certificate of analysis governs), and cites one market-research estimate valuing the US THCV market at $130 million by 2033. The LCB distinguishes Δ9-THCV, which occurs naturally, from Δ8-THCV, which has not been documented to occur naturally and is only semi-synthetically derived, typically by converting CBDV. In Washington's licensed market, only naturally occurring, in-state-extracted THCV is permitted; semi-synthetic and fully synthetic conversion are prohibited, and CBD is the only cannabinoid that may be brought in from outside the licensed system as an additive. THCV is thus a small, supply-constrained ingredient whose legal production method varies by state — the opposite of a commodity input.

What the December 2026 federal hemp dates do to supply

The federal change is a redefinition, not a product-by-product ban list, and it sorts minor-cannabinoid supply by how the molecule was made. Section 781 of Public Law 119-37 rewrote the hemp definition around a 0.3% total-THC standard (counting THCA) and excluded three classes of hemp-derived cannabinoid products, as the Congressional Research Service summarizes in In Focus IF13136: products containing cannabinoids not capable of being naturally produced by the plant; products containing natural cannabinoids that were synthesized or manufactured outside the plant; and finished products above 0.4 mg of combined total THC per container. Products outside the definition are treated as marijuana under the Controlled Substances Act.

The September 2026 delay law (Public Law 119-103, §2019) split the effective dates. On November 12, 2026 — three days after this brief publishes — only the first class loses hemp status: cannabinoids the plant cannot naturally produce at all, with THC-O the clearest case in the legal reporting. CBN, CBG and THCV are all naturally producible, so none of them is in the November group by identity. On December 11, 2026, the rest takes effect: the total-THC test, the 0.4 mg per-container cap, and the exclusion for natural cannabinoids manufactured outside the plant. That second date is the one that reaches mainstream minor-cannabinoid supply, because a large share of hemp-derived CBN, CBG and converted THCV ingredients is produced by converting abundant hemp CBD or CBDV outside the plant rather than by extracting the target molecule directly. A hemp-derived finished product that pairs a minor cannabinoid with THC above the per-container cap also fails on December 11, regardless of how the minor itself was made.

Two uncertainties should be written into supply plans rather than assumed away. First, the statute directed FDA to publish lists of naturally producible and THC-class cannabinoids within 90 days of enactment; as of the Congressional Research Service's September 28, 2026 update, no list had been published, and industry reporting checking again in October 2026 found none — so the natural-versus-converted determination currently rests on the manufacturer's own production records and certificates of analysis. Second, bills to delay, repeal or replace the redefinition remained pending without passage as of mid-October 2026 reporting; December 11 is the planning date. What does not change: products made and sold inside state-licensed cannabis and medical systems are not hemp products, and the redefinition does not alter their status — which is why the licensed channel is the stable home for minor-cannabinoid formulation work in 2027, with hemp-derived supply requalifying ingredient by ingredient on production method.

What this means for product teams

  • Formulate minors as ratio partners in proven formats first. The evidence across BDSA tracking, Headset's October 2026 data and the 2026 launch record points the same way: gummies carry roughly 70–75% of minor-cannabinoid sales, and the winning SKUs name a ratio — THC/CBD/CBN, THC/CBG/THCV, CBD:CBG — rather than a lone minor. Tablets and softgels are the credible second format, as the Ascend and Canopy launches show.
  • Write the production method into every ingredient specification. "Extracted from the plant" and "converted outside the plant" are now different legal goods with different federal dates. Require suppliers to state and document the method — extraction, semi-synthesis from a named precursor, or full synthesis — and keep the batch records with the certificate of analysis.
  • Audit hemp-derived minor supply against December 11, 2026, not November 12. CBN, CBG and THCV are naturally producible, so the November date does not reach them by identity; converted supply and any finished hemp product above 0.4 mg of total THC per container lose hemp status on December 11. Requalify, reformulate, or move affected SKUs into licensed channels before that date.
  • Build THCV plans around scarcity. At typical yields below 0.2% in most plants, THCV is a constrained input: secure cultivar-specific extraction supply, verify content on the batch certificate of analysis rather than the cultivar name, and do not base a Washington or similarly restrictive state's plan on converted THCV, which that state's licensed market prohibits.
  • Use California's CBN isolate lane deliberately — and only as specified. AB 8 preserves CBD and CBN isolate above 99% purity with no THC and no synthetic cannabinoids for foods and beverages in the hemp program. That is a narrow, documented lane, not a general hemp exemption; anything with detectable THC belongs in the licensed system now, ahead of full integration on January 1, 2028.
  • Keep the claims line where the evidence is. Ratio and content labelling is what the market leaders share; outcome claims are not supported by the current human literature, which the Washington LCB describes as limited and largely preclinical for THCV. Differentiate on format fit, ratio clarity and documented sourcing — the attributes a buyer can verify on the label and the certificate of analysis.
