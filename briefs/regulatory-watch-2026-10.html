Regulatory Watch: Two Federal Clocks and California's Hemp Integration

Published October 29, 2026 · Updated November 11, 2026 · CannActuality Research Desk

Correction, November 11, 2026: this brief originally said California's 15% excise tax reached hemp products on January 1, 2026, gave the 15% rate an end date of June 30, 2028, described the rescheduling stay as still running, and called the DEA order's § 280E language unresolved. Those passages are corrected below, with sources.

Key takeaways

  • Already in force: DEA's final order, announced April 23 and effective April 28, 2026, placed FDA-approved marijuana products and marijuana under qualifying state medical licenses into Schedule III, with a federal registration pathway. Adult-use marijuana remains Schedule I under federal law.
  • Undecided: The broader rulemaking to move all marijuana to Schedule III was stayed on September 29, 2026. On October 22 the chief administrative law judge admitted the GAO report and set a short briefing schedule; the stay expired when replies closed on November 5. No recommended decision or final-rule date had been published as of November 11, 2026.
  • November 12, 2026: Cannabinoids the cannabis plant cannot naturally produce lose federal hemp status.
  • December 11, 2026: The rest of the federal hemp redefinition takes effect — a 0.3% total-THC (including THCA) plant standard and a 0.4 mg total-THC cap per finished container — ending the federal basis for today's intoxicating hemp market.
  • California: AB 8's interim rules have applied since January 1, 2026 (hemp flower and pre-rolls banned; until new rules, the licensed channel cannot take hemp inputs or sell hemp products). Full integration into the licensed framework, and the 15% excise on hemp-concentrate products, land January 1, 2028.

Federal clock one: rescheduling split in two

2026 divided federal marijuana policy into a finished piece and an unfinished one. The finished piece: a final order signed April 22, announced April 23 and published and effective April 28, 2026 (91 Fed. Reg. 22714), issued under 21 U.S.C. § 811(d)(1) placing three categories into Schedule III — marijuana within an FDA-approved product; marijuana cultivated and sold under qualifying state medical marijuana licenses; and covered extracts and naturally derived delta-9 THC tied to those channels. The order creates a federal registration pathway (21 C.F.R. § 1301.13(k)) with an expedited six-month review in which a qualifying state medical license is treated as conclusive evidence of authorization. DEA stated expressly that marijuana outside those channels — including all state adult-use activity — remains a Schedule I controlled substance, and that hemp and synthetically derived THC fall outside the order. On taxes, the order states that state medical licensees "will no longer be subject to the deduction disallowance imposed by Section 280E," while also saying nothing in the rule "constitutes a determination regarding federal tax liability." Treasury's April 23 release points the same way: relief generally applies for the full taxable year that includes the effective date, with § 280E still applying to Schedule I activity such as adult-use. What is not settled is formal IRS guidance (listed in the 2026–2027 Priority Guidance Plan, none published as of November 11, 2026), whether DEA registration is required for relief, how mixed businesses apportion expenses, and prior tax years. Operators should still take tax counsel before booking the benefit. On October 30, 2026, DOJ announced the first registrations under the order: more than 900 dispensaries in seven medical-only states.

The unfinished piece: the general rulemaking to move all marijuana from Schedule I to Schedule III. Following Executive Order 14370 (December 2025) directing DOJ to complete the process expeditiously, DEA held merits hearings from June 29 to July 15, 2026. On September 29, 2026, Chief Administrative Law Judge Derek C. Julius stayed the proceedings while he considered admitting a September 23, 2026 GAO report (GAO-26-108623) on federal drug-scheduling process into the record. On October 22 he admitted the report, denied a request to reopen the hearing, and allowed limited briefs due October 29 with replies due November 5; his order said the stay would expire by its own terms at the end of that schedule. As of November 11, 2026 no recommended decision had been published and no final-rule date exists. Roadmaps that assume a completion date are assuming something no source supports.

Federal clock two: the hemp redefinition

Section 781 of Public Law 119-37 (signed November 12, 2025) rewrote the federal definition of hemp. Three changes do the work: the 0.3% plant threshold now counts total THC, including THCA; cannabinoids that are synthesized or cannot be naturally produced by the plant are excluded from hemp entirely; and finished hemp products are capped at 0.4 mg of total THC per container — a level no intoxicating gummy, beverage, or flower product on the market meets. Products outside the new definition are marijuana under the Controlled Substances Act.

The effective dates split in two. Under the original law, everything landed November 12, 2026. The Continuing Appropriations and Extensions Act, 2027 (Public Law 119-103, signed September 2, 2026) moved the main body of the redefinition to December 11, 2026, while the exclusion for non-naturally-producible cannabinoids still takes effect November 12, 2026. As of September 28, 2026, FDA had not yet published the list of naturally occurring and THC-class cannabinoids the law directed it to issue. Bills to delay, repeal, or replace the redefinition with a regulatory framework were pending in committee as of October 2026 — none had passed. State-licensed medical and adult-use programs are not changed by the hemp law; what ends is the unlicensed federal channel.

California: AB 8 integration is already running

California moved first. AB 8 (signed October 2, 2025) phases hemp-derived cannabinoids into the licensed cannabis framework. Interim provisions effective January 1, 2026 ban hemp flower and pre-rolls outright and bar tobacco retailers from selling cannabis or hemp products. Until new rules are adopted, the Department of Cannabis Control (DCC) says licensed manufacturers may use only cannabinoids from licensed sources, licensed distributors cannot transport hemp, and licensed retailers cannot sell hemp products, so moving a hemp product into California's licensed channel today means remaking it from licensed cannabis. On January 1, 2028, full integration takes effect: intoxicating hemp products must move through state-licensed channels with full MAUCRSA compliance — licensing, testing, track-and-trace, and age verification. Separately, the cannabis excise tax rate is 15% of gross receipts on retail sales from October 1, 2025; under the statute the rate can next be adjusted for fiscal year 2028-29, capped at 19%. CDTFA says the excise tax reaches hemp-concentrate products (other than CBD-isolate-only products) from January 1, 2028. That gives licensed operators a stable, if still substantial, tax assumption for 2026–2027 planning. The Governor's 2026-27 budget funds DCC and CDTFA enforcement of the hemp integration, which is the practical signal that the interim rules are being policed, not parked.

What this means for product teams

  • Split your portfolio by legal channel today. Every SKU should be tagged: state-licensed cannabis, state-licensed medical, or hemp-derived unlicensed. The third tag has a federal end date of December 11, 2026 (November 12 for synthetic-cannabinoid products).
  • Hemp brands: choose the licensed conversion or the exit, now. Reformulating under a 0.4 mg/container cap is not a viable path for intoxicating products. The real options are migration into state licensed channels — in California, under AB 8's framework — or an orderly wind-down of affected SKUs.
  • Medical-channel operators: evaluate DEA registration. The April 2026 order's registration pathway and its § 280E language are material to product economics. Until formal IRS guidance is published, model both outcomes.
  • Do not schedule launches against broad rescheduling. The proceeding has no recommended decision and no decision date. Rescheduling is a scenario to plan for, not a milestone to plan around.
  • California roadmaps: design for MAUCRSA from the start. New hemp-derived products aimed at California should be developed to licensed-channel standards — testing, track-and-trace, packaging, age-gated retail — because that is the only channel the state is leaving open, with enforcement funded.
