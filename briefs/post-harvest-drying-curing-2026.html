Post-Harvest Science: Drying, Curing, and Water Activity

Published November 9, 2026 · CannActuality Research Desk · For licensed vendors and product teams

Key takeaways

  • Drying is a chemical step, not just water removal — and it is chemovar-specific. In the Volcani Center controlled-atmosphere trials, a high-THCA chemovar lost 2–20% of its THCA across drying methods while a hybrid chemovar's THCA and CBDA stayed statistically flat under identical conditions (Birenboim et al., Plants, 2024). The same room, run the same way, does not produce the same result for every cultivar.
  • Water activity is the number the system grades. ASTM D8197 sets 0.55–0.65 aw for dry cannabis flower and NIST Handbook 130 carries 0.60 ± 0.05 — yet FTI Consulting's analysis of state laboratory data found all ten states' mean water activity below the range, with 75% of samples at or below the 0.55 floor in all but one state (ATACH, 2026). The industry's systematic error is over-drying, not under-drying.
  • Every accelerated or reordered step trades something measurable. Freeze-drying limited cannabinoid decarboxylation but stripped volatiles: 45 compounds measured lower than tray drying, 28 of them terpenes — including the four compounds that contribute up to half of cannabis aroma (Spadafora et al., 2024). Trim timing is the same kind of fork: mild wet trimming measured highest for cannabinoids, post-drying trimming highest for terpenes (Brikenstein et al., 2024).

Fresh flower is mostly water — and still chemically alive

Freshly harvested inflorescence is 78–80% moisture (Spadafora et al., 2024), and the dry-cure sequence removes most of it: the Volcani trimming trial measured a weight loss of 75 ± 2.6% from harvest to finished flower (Brikenstein et al., 2024). What leaves is not only water. While the tissue stays moist, its own enzymes keep working. In Birenboim et al.'s drying trials (Plants, 2024), the precursor cannabinoid CBGA fell 42% in the high-THCA chemovar and 37% in the hybrid chemovar under open-air drying — with no corresponding rise in the THCA, CBDA or CBCA it normally feeds, a mass balance the authors could not close. Decarboxylation runs during drying too: in the high-THCA chemovar, neutral THC measured roughly 3.5 times its harvest-day level after open-air drying, and in the hybrid, THC and CBD measured about eight and three times harvest levels respectively under the same conditions. A long, uncontrolled dry is therefore not a pause between cultivation and product. It is a second, unmonitored production step.

Speed is a mold control: the controlled-atmosphere evidence

The strongest head-to-head evidence comes from the Agricultural Research Organization's Volcani Center, which dried two commercial chemovars in controlled-atmosphere (CA) chambers — set gas composition, dry gases and desiccant — against traditional open-air drying (Birenboim et al., Plants, 2024). The CA lots reached stable dry weight in six days. The open-air lots had still not stabilized after 14 days: the high-THCA chemovar retained 45% more water than its CA-dried counterpart, and the hybrid 16% more, and both needed an extra day with desiccant to finish — a process the authors put at 2.5 times slower overall, before the two-to-three-week container cure traditional processing then adds. The extra days at high moisture were not benign. By day 12, roughly 80% of the traditionally dried high-THCA inflorescences were highly infested with Alternaria alternata, with low-level Botrytis cinerea also present; Alternaria counts ran one to two orders of magnitude above the CA-dried and fresh samples, and no visible mold was detected in any CA-dried lot. Chemistry tracked the same split: THCA in the high-THCA chemovar fell 2–20% depending on drying condition, worst under open air, while the hybrid chemovar's THCA and CBDA changed less than 10% — not statistically significant — under every condition including open air. Even the optimal gas mixture depended on the target: a 5% CO₂ / 5% O₂ / 90% N₂ atmosphere best preserved monoterpenes in both chemovars, while pure N₂ best preserved sesquiterpenes. The finding is not a recipe. It is that drying conditions are a chemovar-level specification.

Terpenes: the light compounds leave first, on a measured curve

The reference decay curve is old and has never been contradicted. Ross and ElSohly measured terpene losses of 31.0% after one week, 44.8% after one month and 55.2% after three months in air-dried, stored inflorescence versus freshly harvested material (1996 measurements, reported in Bueno et al., Journal of Cannabis Research, 2020). The losses are not uniform across the profile. Monoterpenes — the lightest, most volatile fraction — go first: in Birenboim et al.'s trials, β-myrcene fell 3–20% during drying while α-pinene and d-limonene held stable, and sesquiterpene concentrations rose 20–50% in relative terms as water and monoterpenes left, lifting total measured terpenes roughly 25–30% on a dry-weight basis. A dried sample can therefore test "higher" in total terpenes than the fresh plant while smelling noticeably flatter: the ratio has shifted toward the heavy end. Heat steepens every part of this. Prior hot-air drying work reviewed by Birenboim et al. found monoterpene reductions of 38–95% against fresh inflorescence. The protocols in the studies that preserved chemistry ran cool: 15°C in the Volcani controlled-atmosphere chambers (Brikenstein et al., 2024), and 18–21°C at 50–55% relative humidity for about five days in Spadafora et al.'s traditional tray-drying protocol (2024). The decline continues after the dry: across four months of post-cure storage, THCA fell a further 4–23% depending on storage treatment, and monoterpenes degraded faster than sesquiterpenes throughout (Birenboim et al., Plants, 2024, storage trial).

Wet trim or dry trim is a measured fork, not a preference

Trimming is usually argued from handling lore — fresh trichomes are less brittle, so trim wet; dry flower is easier to manicure, so trim dry. The Volcani group ran the comparison directly on a commercial hybrid chemovar (Brikenstein et al., Medical Cannabis and Cannabinoids, 2024): mild wet trimming (stems removed, sugar leaves cut halfway) before drying, aggressive wet trimming before drying, and trimming only after a six-day dry, all dried at 15°C to a final water content of 10% ± 1%. Mild pre-drying trimming produced the highest cannabinoid concentrations. Post-drying trimming produced the highest terpene content. The authors' conclusion is the operational one: optimizing for both at once appears difficult, so the trim decision is a prioritization decision — cannabinoid-led or aroma-led — and it belongs in the product specification, not in room habit.

The cure: equilibration under seal, with the decay clock running

Curing, as the literature describes it, is the sealed-container phase after drying: inflorescences are held in containers that are periodically aired to release residual moisture, traditionally for two to three weeks, until moisture equalizes through the lot (Birenboim et al., 2024). Its purpose is equilibration, not further bulk drying — a lot that enters the cure unevenly exits it unevenly. The container evidence is thinner than the drying evidence, but Spadafora et al.'s six-month storage trial (18°C, 60% relative humidity) is directly relevant to cure and pack-out choices: flower stored in a sealed glass bottle retained its initial volatile profile better than flower in a closed high-density polyethylene box or on an open tray, though the glass-stored flower also showed more conversion of acidic cannabinoids to their neutral forms. Two disciplines follow. First, the cure endpoint should be set by measured equilibration — moisture and water activity stable across the lot — rather than by a fixed calendar, because every week in the cure sits on the Ross and ElSohly decay curve above. Second, "shelf life" has a working definition in this literature: the period over which the major cannabinoid holds at or above 95% of its post-dry concentration (Birenboim et al., 2024). Cure and storage conditions are what move a lot along that definition.

Water activity: where post-harvest becomes compliance

Water activity (aw) measures the water available for microbial growth and chemical reactions — not the total water present, which is moisture content, a different number that correlates loosely and varies by cultivar and grind (ATACH, 2026). It is the metric the standards system adopted. ASTM D8197 specifies 0.55–0.65 aw for dry cannabis flower, ASTM D8196 sets the measurement practice, and NIST Handbook 130 carries the range as 0.60 ± 0.05 at the point flower is offered for sale or transferred. State cannabis regulators have adopted aw limits inconsistently, but licensed laboratories report the number on certificates of analysis across the major markets — which makes FTI Consulting's analysis for ATACH (2026) the best available picture of where the industry actually lands:

The over-dry side of the window is an economic and quality problem before it is a compliance one. A pound of flower dried from 0.65 to 0.45 aw loses about 18 grams of sellable weight, and flower held at 0.65 aw measured higher terpene concentrations than flower at 0.45 aw in the Cannabis Research Coalition work ATACH cites. The under-dry side is the safety problem: risk of yeast and mold growth rises above 0.65, and Aspergillus — the genus behind most cannabis recalls — is reported to thrive at water activities of 0.83–0.99, far above a compliant cure but reachable inside a sealed, unevenly dried lot where pockets of flower never equilibrated. Between 2019 and 2025, 61% of Colorado Marijuana Enforcement Division health and safety advisories involved unsafe mold or yeast levels (30% named Aspergillus), and 67% of California's mandatory cannabis recalls between September 2024 and December 2025 cited Aspergillus (ATACH, 2026). Water activity control governs whether organisms present can grow; it does not remediate contamination a lot already carries — which is why the drying-room evidence above, where slow open-air drying multiplied Alternaria counts, and the aw evidence here are the same story told at two points in the process.

Freeze-drying and the other shortcuts

Freeze-drying is the accelerated method with the most confident marketing and the clearest head-to-head result against it. The process freezes flower below −40°C and removes water by sublimation under vacuum, finishing in 24–48 hours (Spadafora et al., Analytical and Bioanalytical Chemistry, 2024). Against traditional tray drying, freeze-drying did preserve the cannabinoid side of the ledger — it limited decarboxylation of the acidic cannabinoids. But of 149 metabolites profiled, 45 measured significantly lower in freeze-dried flower, and 28 of the 45 were terpenes. The most affected compounds — β-myrcene, α-pinene, d-limonene and terpinolene — are, in the authors' accounting, responsible for up to 50% of the aroma of cannabis. The mechanism is physical: ice crystals damage the microstructure of the trichomes, and the volatiles they held escape under vacuum. The authors' conclusion is unusually direct — freeze-drying "does not represent an optimal alternative" where both cannabinoids and aroma compounds matter. Heat-driven acceleration fares no better in the literature Birenboim et al. review: hot-air, infrared and microwave drying cut drying time sharply, but one reviewed study measured reductions of more than 45% in CBDA, CBGA, total CBD and total cannabinoid content versus fresh inflorescence across all three methods. The accelerated approach with preservation evidence behind it is the controlled-atmosphere chamber: six days instead of fourteen-plus, no detected mold, and terpene preservation at or above harvest levels in the chemovars tested — at the cost of equipment and per-chemovar validation rather than chemistry.

How post-harvest variance becomes finished-product variance

Read the studies together and the certificate of analysis stops looking like a property of the cultivar. The same genetics, dried two ways, produced THCA outcomes ranging from a 20% loss to no significant change, neutral-THC multiples from about 3.5× to 8× harvest levels, and terpene profiles rebalanced toward sesquiterpenes by double-digit percentages. A lot tested at pack-out and the same lot tested after three months of storage sit 55% apart on the terpene decay curve. Water activity at the lab ranged across state medians from 0.41 to 0.51 — all for flower sold under the same product category. None of that variance was created in the grow room. For product teams, the practical translation is that "the cultivar tests at X" is not a specification; the specification is the cultivar plus a defined dry, a defined cure endpoint, a defined aw at pack-out, and a defined time-to-test. Where any of those four is left to room practice, it re-enters the product as batch-to-batch variance that no downstream formulation step can remove.

What this means for product teams

  • Specify the drying endpoint in water activity, not days. Target the ASTM D8197 window of 0.55–0.65 aw, measured by the D8196 practice, at pack-out — and audit the distribution, not just the mean. FTI's state data (medians 0.41–0.51) shows what calendar-based drying produces at scale: systematic over-drying that costs roughly 18 g of sellable weight per pound between 0.65 and 0.45 aw, plus terpene content.
  • Validate the dry per chemovar before locking an SOP. Under identical conditions, one Volcani chemovar lost up to 20% of its THCA while another's major cannabinoids did not move significantly, and the best-performing gas mixture differed by compound class. Run harvest-day versus post-dry retention checks for each cultivar and treat the drying curve as part of the cultivar's specification sheet.
  • Treat trim timing as a formulation decision. In the only direct comparison, mild wet trimming measured highest for cannabinoid content and post-drying trimming highest for terpene content (Brikenstein et al., 2024). Decide per SKU which the product is — cannabinoid-led or aroma-led — and write the trim protocol to match, instead of running one house method for everything.
  • Price freeze-drying by what it actually buys. It buys 24–48-hour turnaround and preservation of acidic cannabinoid forms. It costs the volatile fraction — 28 terpenes measured lower, including compounds contributing up to half of aroma (Spadafora et al., 2024). That trade can be right for extraction-bound or acid-form product; for aroma-led flower, vendor retention claims are not what the head-to-head evidence found.
  • Set the cure endpoint by equilibration, then package like it matters. The cure's job is moisture equalization across the lot; sealed glass retained the volatile profile best over six months in Spadafora et al.'s storage trial, ahead of HDPE and open storage. Uneven lots sealed early are where localized high-aw pockets — and the Aspergillus risk that follows them — come from.
  • Write time-to-test into the product spec. Terpene content falls 31% in the first week and 55% by three months on the classic decay curve, and THCA fell a further 4–23% over four months of storage. A COA is a point on a curve: define when in the post-harvest timeline your label values are measured, and manage shelf life against the 95%-of-initial benchmark the preservation literature uses.
