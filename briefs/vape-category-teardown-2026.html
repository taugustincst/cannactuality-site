Vape Category Teardown 2026: Hardware, Flavor Systems, and the Disposables Question

Published November 11, 2026 · CannActuality Research Desk · For licensed vendors and product teams

Key takeaways

  • Vape is an $8 billion US category, about 26% of cannabis retail dollars, with sales up 6% in the 12 months ending May 2026 (BDSA). In California it is effectively tied with flower: Headset's October 2026 data puts vapor pens at $101.4 million, up 10.6% year over year, against $103.2 million for flower.
  • Disposables (all-in-ones) became the majority format nationally for the first time: they grew 41% to $3.8 billion while cartridges fell 14% to $4.1 billion, and passed 50% of vape dollars in April 2026. California is a notable exception: cartridges still hold 54% of vape dollars there, and 2-gram disposables are not permitted in the state.
  • Oil is moving up the premium ladder. Live resin and rosin together reached about a third of vape revenue, up from roughly 28%; live resin reached 28% of vape sales in May 2026 while distillate lost share at roughly flat dollars (BDSA).
  • Flavor is a compliance decision before it is a marketing one. California allows only terpenes consistent with those naturally present in cannabis and bans flavors such as mango or menthol; Washington and New York take similar botanical-but-cannabis-like approaches, and California is funding a reference dataset to help tell the difference.
  • California's new ban on disposable vapes (AB 762) excludes cannabis devices after a January 2026 amendment, but California cannabis packaging still may not describe a vape as disposable or trash-safe, and hazardous-waste disposal messaging is required in advertising.

The category in one view

Vapor pens are the second-largest cannabis category in the US and are closing on flower in the markets where they are strongest. BDSA's 2026 vape report, covering June 2025 to May 2026, sizes the category at $8 billion, about 26% of cannabis retail, with dollar sales up 6% year over year and share flat. Headset's Q4 2025 report puts vapor pens at 25.5% of US sales, second to flower at 38.8%.

California is where the format question matters most. Headset measured vapor pens at 29.8% of California sales in Q4 2025, near the top of US markets. BDSA's May 2026 snapshot has vape ahead of flower in California, 31% to 30%, and MJBizDaily, citing Department of Cannabis Control (DCC) data, reported in March 2026 that vapes first outsold flower in California in June 2025. Headset's California market data for October 2026 shows the two still trading places: flower at $103.2 million (up 8.4%) and vapor pens at $101.4 million (up 10.6%). Across trackers and windows, vape in California is now a co-lead category.

Younger buyers drive the growth. In Headset's California data for January to August 2026, Gen Z vapor pen spending rose 3.9% while Millennial spending fell 0.7%. Nationally, Headset attributes 32% of vapor pen dollars to Gen Z.

Format: the disposable crossover, with a California caveat

The headline format shift is complete at national level. In BDSA's 12-month window, disposables grew 41% to $3.8 billion and cartridges fell 14% to $4.1 billion. Disposables were 42.9% of vape dollars in June 2025, passed 50% in April 2026 and reached 51.1% in May 2026. Headset's data for October 2025 to September 2026 shows the same parity, 50% each.

Fill size is the second format lever. The 2-gram disposable rose from about 16% to 35% of disposable dollars in two years, while the 1-gram held at about 53% (BDSA). In May 2026 the 2-gram format took 94% of disposable dollars in Oklahoma and 73% in Arizona. Headset's 2025 data shows 2-gram products up 95.8% year over year in the US while half-gram products fell 40.5%; the 1-gram size still accounted for 75.1% of US vapor pen sales. Because BDSA reports that 2-gram disposables are not currently permitted in California, the largest market's disposable line has to be built below that size.

Hardware architecture: 510 dominates, closed systems are a niche

For cartridges, the 510 thread is the industry standard. Headset's February 2026 report found 510-threaded hardware accounted for 91.3% of US vapor pen sales and 99.8% in Canada; the leading proprietary pod system held about 5% of US sales. A closed pod system is therefore a brand strategy, not a category default.

Oil type: distillate still leads, live resin takes the growth

Distillate remains the largest oil type but is losing share. Headset puts distillate at 56.7% of US vapor pen sales through 2025, declining. BDSA reports that live resin and rosin together reached about a third of vape revenue over the past year, up from roughly 28%, while distillate held roughly flat in dollars. Within that premium tier:

  • Live resin: about $2.1 billion and 26% of vape sales over the 12 months, rising to 28% in May 2026 (BDSA). Headset describes it as the clear number-two extract type.
  • Rosin: about $442 million, around 5.5% of vape sales, up about 19% year over year (BDSA).
  • Diamonds: up more than 60% year over year in both the US and Canada in Headset's 2025 data, but still a low-single-digit share in the US against 29.7% of Canadian sales.

Flavor systems: what states actually allow

Most markets now draw the line not between flavored and unflavored, but between flavors that resemble cannabis and flavors that do not.

  • California. Under DCC regulation section 17303.1, effective November 7, 2022, inhaled products may contain only cannabis, cannabis concentrate, terpenes and inactive ingredients the FDA permits for inhalation. DCC guidance states that artificial or synthetic terpenes are not permitted, that flavors not naturally occurring in cannabis are prohibited, and names examples including menthol, mint, mango, strawberry, grape, vanilla and bubblegum. Non-cannabis-derived terpenes are allowed only when they are consistent with terpenes that naturally exist in cannabis. All ingredients, including added non-cannabis components, must be listed on the label, and noncompliant products may be considered adulterated.
  • Washington. RCW 69.50.327 allows flavors derived from botanical terpenes naturally occurring in the cannabis plant, provided the flavor mimics a cannabis terpene profile, excludes synthetic terpenes, and lets the Board prohibit specific flavors by rule.
  • Other markets. A Washington State Liquor and Cannabis Board research brief (August 2024, updated February 2025) summarizes New York as requiring botanical sources, banning youth-appealing flavors and capping total terpenes at 10%; New Jersey as capping total terpenes at 10%; and Connecticut as requiring flavors and terpenes to come from cannabis grown and regulated in-state. Canada limits inhalable extracts to the flavor of cannabis.

Enforcement in California is about to get a technical basis. The DCC is funding a two-year, $1.23 million research grant to Terpene Belt Farms with UCLA, UC Davis and S3 Collective to build a reference dataset of terpenes and volatile compounds that occur naturally in California cannabis flower, so regulators can judge whether flavors in inhalable products come from the plant (The Marijuana Herald, February 2026).

Hardware compliance: test the finished device

Hardware is part of the tested product. California requires testing for cadmium, lead, arsenic and mercury, with action levels for inhalable cannabis goods of 0.2, 0.5, 0.2 and 0.1 micrograms per gram respectively (Title 4 CCR section 15723, as amended effective November 7, 2022). Michigan added copper and nickel to its required testing from July 1, 2020.

Timing of the test is now an enforcement issue. In May 2026 Missouri's Division of Cannabis Regulation recalled hundreds of live resin and live rosin cartridges and all-in-one products because the concentrates had been sampled and tested before being filled into hardware; the division's rules require sampling after processing into vape cartridges. In California, a July 2026 DCC recall for pesticide contamination (pyrethrins) covered 0.5-gram and 1-gram cartridges and a 1-gram integrated vaporizer from a single oil batch, a reminder that one oil lot can carry compliance risk across every format it fills.

The disposables question: e-waste and disposal rules

California's new disposable vape ban does not reach cannabis, but cannabis vapes are not unregulated on disposal.

  • AB 762 (signed September 2026). Bars the manufacture and import of disposable, battery-embedded vapor devices from January 1, 2027 and their sale from January 1, 2028. A device counts as disposable if it cannot be recharged or cannot be refilled. The law covers non-cannabis devices; Cal NORML reported that the bill, introduced to cover both nicotine and cannabis disposables, was amended in January 2026 to remove cannabis vapes.
  • AB 1894 (effective July 1, 2024). For cannabis cartridges and integrated vaporizers, all advertising and marketing must display a message that the spent device should be disposed of as hazardous waste at a household hazardous waste or other approved facility. Packaging and labels may not indicate or imply that the product is disposable, may be thrown in the trash or may be added to recycling streams.
  • Producer responsibility is arriving elsewhere. Maine's LD 1519, reported in June 2026 as the first US extended producer responsibility program for vapes and e-cigarettes, requires manufacturers to join a producer responsibility organization by November 1, 2027. The reporting did not say whether cannabis devices are covered.

What this means for product teams

  • Set the format mix by state. A disposable-led line fits Arizona, New York and Michigan, where disposables hold 64% to 79% of vape dollars. In California, Florida and Pennsylvania, cartridges still hold the majority. A national format plan will be wrong in at least one large market.
  • Default to 510 unless the closed system is the brand. At 91.3% of US sales, 510 hardware is the format shoppers already own. Choose a proprietary pod only if the business case rests on lock-in and can win share from a roughly 5% segment.
  • Plan fill sizes around legal ceilings. The 2-gram disposable is the fastest-growing size where it is allowed, but California does not permit it. Build the line so the 1-gram is the anchor and the 2-gram is a state-specific extension.
  • Put live resin in the premium tier and keep distillate as the base. Live resin is at 26% to 28% of vape sales and still gaining; rosin is a smaller, growing tier. Distillate holds the volume but is losing share, so new premium SKUs should name their oil type.
  • Write flavor specs to the strictest rule you sell under. Use cannabis-derived terpenes, or botanical terpenes that match cannabis profiles, list every ingredient, and avoid fruit, dessert or candy flavor names. Expect California's reference dataset to raise the evidence bar for botanical blends.
  • Qualify hardware as part of the product. Ask suppliers for materials documentation covering the metals your states test for, including copper and nickel in Michigan, and test oil after it is filled into the device. Missouri's 2026 recall shows that testing the concentrate alone may not count.
  • Remove the word "disposable" from California packaging. AB 1894 bars packaging that implies a vape can be thrown away and requires hazardous-waste disposal messaging in advertising. Rechargeable, refillable or take-back designs reduce exposure if producer responsibility rules expand to cannabis.
