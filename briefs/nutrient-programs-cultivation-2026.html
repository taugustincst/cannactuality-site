Nutrient Programs and Mineral Nutrition: What the Evidence Supports

Published November 9, 2026 · CannActuality Research Desk · For licensed vendors and product teams

Key takeaways

  • Across the controlled dose-response literature, one pattern repeats for nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium: cannabinoid and terpenoid concentrations are highest at or below the supply level that maximizes yield, and fall as supply rises. THCA concentration dropped 69% as nitrogen supply increased from 30 to 320 mg/L in the Volcani Center trials. A nutrient program is a chemotype decision, not only a yield decision.
  • More feed is not more flower. In Utah State closed-system hydroponic trials, raising phosphorus from 15 to 90 mg/L and doubling solution EC from 2 to 4 mS/cm changed neither yield nor cannabinoid quality — the extra phosphorus accumulated in leaf and flower tissue and in the root-zone solution instead.
  • Magnesium runs the other way: it is the one macronutrient tested whose deficiency lowers cannabinoid and terpene production, with an optimum at 35 mg/L. And the industry's most universal finishing practice, pre-harvest flushing, is the best-tested and least supported — a Guelph thesis, an industry trial and a five-cultivar peer-reviewed study all find no meaningful change in flower mineral content, potency or terpene profile.

Nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium: concentration and yield pull in opposite directions

Almost everything known from controlled cannabis nutrition research comes from a small number of programs — the Volcani Center in Israel, the University of Guelph, and Utah State University — working in soilless media under controlled environments. Their shared method is the dose-response trial: hold everything constant, vary one element, and measure both biomass and chemistry. Read together, the macronutrient trials tell a consistent story. The supply level that maximizes inflorescence yield is at or above the level that maximizes cannabinoid and terpenoid concentration, so every feed chart embeds a trade-off between the two.

Nitrogen is the nutrient cannabis consumes in the largest quantity, and the most studied. In the Volcani trials, plants were grown at five nitrogen levels from 30 to 320 mg/L. Yield rose with supply up to the 160–320 mg/L range, but cannabinoid and terpenoid concentrations fell as nitrogen increased — THCA concentration decreased by 69% between the 30 and 320 mg/L treatments. The lab's conclusion, at both the vegetative and flowering stages, is an optimum of 160 mg/L: high enough for yield, low enough to limit dilution of secondary metabolites. The form of nitrogen matters as much as the amount. In a 2022 Frontiers in Plant Science trial at a uniform 200 mg/L, shifting the ammonium share of nitrogen from 0% to 50% cut inflorescence yield by 46%; ratios of 10–30% ammonium were tolerated with small penalties, 50% produced toxicity symptoms that appeared only late in maturation, and 100% ammonium killed the plants. Secondary metabolite production was highest under all-nitrate nutrition.

Phosphorus shows the same trade-off at much lower numbers. Shiponi and Bernstein grew two cultivars at 5, 15, 30, 60 and 90 mg/L: supply at or below 15 mg/L was insufficient for optimal plant function, 30–90 mg/L was the optimal range for development, and 30 mg/L already produced 80% of maximum yield. About 80% of plant phosphorus accumulated in the inflorescences. Yet P supply above 5 mg/L reduced THCA and CBDA concentrations by up to 25%, and concentrations fell linearly as yield rose — a dilution effect, since total cannabinoid content per plant still increased. A Utah State trial in closed-system hydroponics pushed the point further: raising P input from 15 to 90 mg/L doubled leaf P concentration and raised flower P concentration by 70%, with no difference in yield or quality at any level. Cannabis takes up phosphorus beyond its requirement and stores it in the flower; the excess is cost and waste-stream load, not potency.

Potassium completes the pattern. At five supply levels from 15 to 240 mg/L, the 15 mg/L treatment caused visible chlorosis and reduced yield in both cultivars tested; 60–175 mg/L gave optimal plant function and high yield; and 240 mg/L produced over-supply symptoms in one of the two cultivars. The concentrations of most cannabinoids and terpenoids declined as K supply rose, leading the authors to suggest 60 mg/L — the bottom of the functional range — as the application level that keeps yield and secondary metabolism high together. A Guelph response-surface analysis of NPK combinations points the same way: inflorescence yield responded to elevated nitrogen and phosphorus, with no yield response to potassium supply at all.

EC and pH: wide tolerance, narrow returns

The Utah State group grew medical cannabis in closed deep-flow hydroponics at solution electrical conductivities of 2 and 4 mS/cm, crossed with the phosphorus levels above. Doubling nutrient input from 2 to 4 mS/cm increased nutrient accumulation in the solution but not yield or quality, and even the highest root-zone concentrations produced no leaf necrosis. Cannabis, on this evidence, tolerates strong solutions — it simply does not reward them. The operational EC signal in the Volcani work is subtler: leachate EC ran steady at 7–16% above the irrigation solution across treatments, which is a monitoring benchmark for root-zone salt accumulation, not a reason to raise input strength.

Solution pH in the controlled literature is strikingly uniform. The Volcani phosphorus trial held pH at 5.5–6.0, the ammonium/nitrate trial adjusted its solution to 5.6–6.0, and the magnesium trial adjusted to 5.8. No controlled cannabis study in this literature establishes an optimum outside that band, and one mechanism argues for holding it steady: roots taking up ammonium release hydrogen ions and acidify the rhizosphere, while nitrate uptake alkalinizes it, so the nitrogen-form ratio is itself a pH-management variable. The micronutrient side of the research recipes is similarly modest — the ammonium/nitrate trial's published solution carried iron at 1.31 mg/L, manganese 0.6, zinc 0.3, copper 0.13, boron 0.1 and molybdenum 0.003 mg/L alongside its macronutrients.

Calcium, magnesium, silicon: strong evidence for one, thin evidence for two

Magnesium is the outlier in the macronutrient set, and the reason is biochemical: Mg is required for the formation of geranyl diphosphate (GPP), the shared precursor of cannabinoids and terpenes, and for the activity of key enzymes in both biosynthesis pathways. In the Volcani reproductive-stage trial at 2, 20, 35, 70 and 140 mg/L, low supply of 2–20 mg/L restricted plant function and metabolite concentrations; 35 mg/L was optimal for both yield and metabolite production; and over-supply at 70–140 mg/L cut reproductive biomass by 12.5% while leaving secondary metabolism unharmed. Where N, P and K deficiency raises cannabinoid concentration (in smaller plants), Mg deficiency lowers it — the lab describes the response as unique among the macronutrients it has tested. At the vegetative stage, the optimal Mg range was 35–70 mg/L.

Calcium has no equivalent dose-response trial in the controlled cannabis literature — a genuine evidence gap, given that Ca appears in every research recipe (at 57 mg/L in the ammonium/nitrate trial's solution and 110 mg/L in the magnesium trial's). What the literature does establish is the interaction: K, Ca and Mg compete significantly for root uptake, and ammonium uptake inhibits the uptake of all three cations. A program that pushes any one cation hard — a potassium "booster," a heavy ammonium share — can manufacture a deficiency of the others. Silicon, a beneficial but non-essential element, sits between evidence and marketing. A 2026 review in Agriculture classifies cannabis as an intermediate silicon accumulator that deposits silica in bast fibers and trichomes, with the clearest benefits in propagation and vegetative growth and under stress: root-zone silicon lowered cadmium and zinc uptake in hemp work, and silicon suppressed powdery mildew while raising tissue silicon about 2.1-fold and inflorescence biomass about 1.2-fold without reducing cannabinoid or terpene quality — though that yield effect was only marginally significant and the silicon was co-supplied with a phosphite component. The review's central caution is that no peer-reviewed study has yet isolated silicon's effect on cannabinoid or terpene biosynthesis under standardized flower-production conditions.

Delivery strategy: fertigation precision versus organic complexity

Every dose-response result above exists because mineral fertigation into inert media — perlite, in the Volcani work — makes the dose knowable to the milligram. That precision is itself the finding: chemotype responds measurably to single-element changes, and the cannabinoid profile also varies between plants and within a single plant, so batch consistency is pursued by narrowing every input that can be narrowed. Organic programs trade some of that precision for biological complexity, and the best-controlled organic study shows the cost clearly. Caplan, Dixon and Zheng at the University of Guelph applied a liquid organic fertilizer at rates supplying 57 to 283 mg/L nitrogen to flowering cannabis in two coir-based substrates. The optimum moved with the medium: in the lower-container-capacity substrate, rates supplying 212–261 mg/L maximized yield and cannabinoid yield, while in the higher-capacity substrate the highest rate tested, 283 mg/L, maximized yield but lower rates gave higher cannabinoid concentrations. The substrate alone mattered independently — the lower-capacity mix produced 11% higher floral dry weight, 20% higher THC concentration and 57% higher THC yield per plant — and in both substrates, raising the fertilizer rate increased growth and yield while diluting THC, THCA and CBGA concentrations.

For living-soil systems specifically, the controlled literature is effectively silent: no study reviewed here compares living-soil against fertigated production on chemotype consistency. What can be said from the evidence is structural. In a fertigated system, a nutrient program is a written specification, auditable against a certificate of analysis. In an organic system, the Guelph results show the specification has to be re-derived for each substrate, because the medium changes both the optimum rate and the outcome. Neither approach changes the underlying dose-response biology; they differ in how precisely the dose can be held.

Flushing: the best-tested claim in cultivation, and the weakest result

Pre-harvest flushing — stopping mineral feed and irrigating with plain water for the final days before harvest, on the theory that it clears minerals from the flower — is the one cultivation practice with three independent lines of controlled evidence, and they agree. A University of Guelph thesis on irrigation management (Stemeroff) tested flushing directly and concluded it "was found to be ineffective in removing any significant amount of nutrient from the bud." The most-cited industry trial, run by RX Green Technologies in 2019, grew 48 Cherry Diesel plants in coco and flushed for 14, 10, 7 or 0 days: cured flower weight averaged 97.3 g per plant, THC averaged 21.9%, and total terpenes ran 1.46% to 1.64%, with no statistically significant difference on any measure, and no significant overall change in flower mineral content (nitrogen was 6.7% lower after 14 days, while iron and zinc were actually higher in the longer flushes, consistent with plain water releasing accumulated micronutrients from the media). A blind panel of industry experts found no significant difference in flavor, smoke harshness or ash color; smoothness ratings, if anything, trended toward the unflushed flower. The trial's one real effect was in extraction: trim from the 10- and 14-day flushes yielded about two percentage points more extract (18.2% and 18.0% versus 16.2% unflushed), while the unflushed trim produced the clearest wax. RX Green's trial was industry-run and single-cultivar, not peer-reviewed — but the peer-reviewed test that followed reached the same place. Saloner, Sade and Bernstein (2024) compared flushing against feeding to harvest across five medical cannabis cultivars: flushing changed terpenoid concentrations in 3% of samples and cannabinoid concentrations in 30%, and where cannabinoids did move, they moved in both directions — up in two cultivars, down in one. Leaf fade during a flush is real; it is chlorophyll breaking down in the leaf, and on this evidence it is not a change in the flower. Flushing is a harvest-logistics and extraction decision, not a quality lever — and, as the RX Green report itself notes, it is not a substitute for managing root-zone salts during the cycle, which is a separate job done with runoff and feed adjustment.

What this means for product teams

  • Specify feed programs by element and stage, not by brand recipe. The published optima from the dose-response literature — nitrogen near 160 mg/L, phosphorus near 30 mg/L, potassium near 60 mg/L, magnesium near 35 mg/L in solution culture — sit well below many commercial feed charts. Require cultivation partners and nutrient suppliers to state solution concentrations per element, per growth stage, so a program can be compared against the evidence rather than against a label.
  • Treat a feed change as a chemotype change. Because cannabinoid and terpenoid concentrations fall as N, P and K supply rises past the functional optimum, changing the nutrient program changes the product specification. Lock the feed program into batch specs, and re-validate against certificates of analysis whenever the program, the nitrogen-form ratio, or the substrate changes.
  • Do not pay for EC headroom or phosphorus loading. Doubling solution strength from 2 to 4 mS/cm bought no yield or quality, and phosphorus fed at 90 mg/L rather than 15 mg/L showed up as tissue loading and leachate, not flower. Excess P is a fertilizer cost and a wastewater exposure; the evidence supports feeding to the requirement and monitoring leachate EC instead.
  • Manage the cation triangle as one system. Potassium, calcium and magnesium compete for root uptake, and ammonium suppresses all three. Single-element boosters can induce the deficiency they claim to fix. Magnesium deserves the opposite care: keep it sufficient (the trial optimum was 35 mg/L), because Mg deficiency is the one macronutrient shortage shown to cut both yield and cannabinoid/terpene production.
  • Retire flushing as a quality claim. If a cultivation partner flushes, the defensible reasons are extraction yield and harvest logistics, not flower chemistry — three controlled tests find no meaningful change in mineral content, potency or terpenes. Do not build product quality narratives, or label copy, on a pre-harvest flush.
  • Price the evidence gaps into vendor claims. There is no controlled calcium dose-response for cannabis, no isolated silicon dose-response for cannabinoid or terpene production, and no controlled living-soil-versus-fertigation comparison on chemotype consistency. Input products marketed on those claims are ahead of the published literature; silicon's supported role today is crop resilience — mildew suppression and stress tolerance — not potency.
