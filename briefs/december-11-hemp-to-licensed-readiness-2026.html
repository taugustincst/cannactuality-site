The December 11 Readiness Brief: Converting Hemp SKUs to Licensed Channels

Published November 11, 2026 · CannActuality Research Desk · For licensed vendors and product teams

Key takeaways

  • Two federal dates, one direction. Hemp-derived products with cannabinoids the plant cannot naturally produce lose hemp status on November 12, 2026. The rest of the redefinition, including the 0.3% total-THC standard (THCA counted) and the 0.4 mg total-THC cap per container, takes effect December 11, 2026 (Congressional Research Service).
  • California's licensed channel does not take hemp yet. Until new rules are adopted, the Department of Cannabis Control (DCC) says licensed manufacturers may use only cannabinoids from licensed sources, licensed distributors cannot transport hemp, and licensed retailers cannot sell hemp products. Hemp entry into the licensed supply chain begins January 1, 2028.
  • So "conversion" in California means reformulation. A hemp SKU becomes a licensed SKU by being remade from licensed-source cannabis by a DCC-licensed manufacturer, then tested, tracked, packaged, labeled and taxed like any other cannabis product.
  • Other states split three ways. Illinois folds intoxicating hemp into its cannabis law on November 12, 2026; Washington and New York already route detectable-THC products through licensees; Texas and Florida have no adult-use channel to migrate into.
  • Still open: FDA's cannabinoid lists had not been published as of October 28, 2026, bills to delay or repeal the redefinition had not passed, and California's integration rules are still being written.

The federal clock, re-verified

Section 781 of Public Law 119-37 rewrote the federal hemp definition around a total-THC concentration "including tetrahydrocannabinolic acid" of not more than 0.3% on a dry weight basis, and excluded several classes of product: those with cannabinoids not capable of being naturally produced by the plant, those with natural cannabinoids synthesized or manufactured outside the plant, and final products containing more than 0.4 mg combined total THC (and similar-effect cannabinoids) per container. CRS notes that "container" means the innermost packaging in direct contact with the product. Excluded products are classified as marijuana under the Controlled Substances Act.

The Continuing Appropriations and Extensions Act, 2027 (Public Law 119-103) delayed most of those amendments to December 11, 2026. The exclusion for cannabinoids the plant cannot naturally produce stays on November 12, 2026. CRS's October 28, 2026 Legal Sidebar reports that FDA had published none of the four items the law required (lists of naturally occurring, THC-class and similar-effect cannabinoids, plus guidance on "container"), and that bills to delay the definition by two more years (H.R. 7024, H.R. 7010, S. 3686) or to repeal it (H.R. 6209) were pending. None had been enacted as of that date. Plan to December 11.

California first: what the licensed channel actually requires

AB 8 (Chapter 248, Statutes of 2025) was signed October 2, 2025, and most provisions took effect January 1, 2026. Since then, hemp flower and hemp pre-rolls cannot be sold in California, and the DCC lists five product types as prohibited: alcoholic beverages containing hemp cannabinoids, inhalable hemp products (pre-rolls, vape cartridges, integrated vaporizers), hemp flower, products with synthetic cannabinoids, and products made outside the regulated market. Food, beverages and dietary supplements made with industrial hemp extract may not contain THC or synthetic cannabinoids; the one remaining ingredient lane is CBD or CBN isolate above 99% purity, which still needs a CDPH registration. Tobacco retailers may not possess or sell cannabis at their locations (Business and Professions Code § 22980.6, per Kight Law).

The important detail for migration planning is the DCC's interim position: licensed manufacturers "can only use cannabinoids from licensed sources," licensed distributors "cannot transport hemp," and licensed retailers "cannot sell hemp products." Full integration on January 1, 2028 changes that. From then, distributors may bring hemp plant material into the supply chain under cannabis rules, anyone making intoxicating cannabinoid products in California needs a DCC manufacturing license, and retailers may sell products containing cannabis, hemp or both.

A brand moving into California's licensed market before 2028 is therefore entering the existing MAUCRSA system, not a hemp variant of it:

  • Inputs: cannabinoids sourced from licensed operators only; no hemp-derived or synthetic inputs.
  • Manufacturing: production by a DCC-licensed manufacturer.
  • Testing: every batch tested by a licensed laboratory before sale, covering cannabinoids and terpenes, residual solvents and processing chemicals, pesticides, heavy metals, microbial impurities, mycotoxins, moisture content and water activity, and foreign material. Labs upload the certificate of analysis to track-and-trace within one day of finishing a batch.
  • Distribution: a licensed distributor arranges testing and may transport goods to retail only if they pass; Type 11 distributors also run a quality-assurance review.
  • Track-and-trace: California's system runs on Metrc, and the label must carry the system-issued UID.
  • Labeling: primary panel with product identity, net weight or volume, and the universal symbol (at least 0.5 inch tall), plus "Cannabis-Infused" on edibles; informational panel with the government warning, UID, batch or lot number, packaging date, ingredients and licensee contact; THC and CBD content per package (and per serving where applicable).
  • Excise tax: 15% of gross receipts on retail sales, effective October 1, 2025 (CDTFA). The rate can next be adjusted for fiscal year 2028-29, capped at 19%. CDTFA says the excise tax reaches hemp-concentrate products (other than CBD-isolate-only products) from January 1, 2028.

The state is building the 2028 bridge now. The DCC's January 9, 2026 budget change proposal asks for 22 positions and $7.3 million in 2026-27, with rulemaking to start in 2026-27 and finish in 2027-28. It also seeks $800,000 for Metrc changes to allow hemp entry and exit, which it estimates will take about 12 months, and new laboratory capacity to detect synthetic cannabinoids. A budget request is not an enacted rule, so the 2028 details remain pending.

What is uncertain or pending

  • FDA's lists. Until FDA publishes which cannabinoids are naturally producible and THC-class, the November 12 versus December 11 sorting rests on manufacturers' own records.
  • Congress. Two-year delay bills and a repeal bill were pending as of October 28, 2026. None had passed.
  • California's 2028 rules. Distributor hemp endorsements, hemp testing and labeling, and Metrc entry and exit are still in rulemaking.
  • Michigan and New York. Michigan's hemp package and New York's transition approach had not been resolved in the sources reviewed.

What this means for product teams

  • Inventory your hemp SKUs against both dates. Flag anything with non-naturally-producible cannabinoids for November 12 and everything above 0.4 mg total THC per container for December 11.
  • In California, budget a reformulation, not a relabel. The licensed channel accepts only licensed-source cannabinoids until 2028, so plan new input sourcing, a licensed manufacturer, fresh batch testing and a new label built around the Metrc UID.
  • Re-run unit economics with the 15% excise. It applies to licensed retail sales now and is scheduled to reach hemp-concentrate products in 2028.
  • Pick states by entry mechanism. New York's branding license and Illinois's new processor and infuser licenses are possible routes for brands; Washington and Colorado do not admit hemp-derived THC; Texas and Florida offer no adult-use channel.
  • Keep production-method records with every certificate of analysis. Washington bans synthetic conversion, Colorado treats hemp-derived inputs as a violation, and the federal sorting depends on how each cannabinoid was made.
  • Watch three dockets: FDA's cannabinoid lists, the federal delay bills, and DCC's AB 8 rulemaking.
