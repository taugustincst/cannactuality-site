Lighting Science for Cultivation: Spectrum, Intensity, and Product Consistency

Published November 9, 2026 · CannActuality Research Desk · For licensed vendors and product teams

Key takeaways

  • Intensity is the yield dial, not the potency dial. In the Guelph trials, dry inflorescence yield rose linearly with canopy PPFD up to 1,800 µmol·m⁻²·s⁻¹, and a high-THC cultivar produced 1.6 times the inflorescence dry weight at 1,000 versus 600 µmol·m⁻²·s⁻¹ — while cannabinoid concentration did not move with intensity in either study. Grams are bought with photons; percentage points are set mainly by genetics and spectrum.
  • Spectrum moves chemistry at the margins, and the direction depends on the cultivar. Blue-rich light raised CBGA, the central cannabinoid precursor, in the Bernstein group's work; LED spectra raised THC and CBD concentrations relative to HPS in a repeated head-to-head trial, while HPS produced more flower mass; and end-of-day far-red raised THC concentration in two high-THC lines. No spectrum recipe transfers across cultivars untested.
  • UV is the most oversold dial in the grow room. Two controlled programs found no change in inflorescence cannabinoid content from added UV. The one positive result is narrow: low-dose, UVA-dominant light lifted linalool by 29%, limonene by 25% and myrcene by 22%, with yield and cannabinoid concentration unchanged (Huebner et al., 2024).
  • Photoperiod is a consistency variable, not a fixed constant. Across three medicinal cultivars, schedules starting at 14 hours of light produced the highest flower biomass in every line — but a static 14-hour day significantly reduced THC concentration in the two THC lines while raising the CBD line's total CBD yield by 50–100% (Peterswald et al., 2023). Batch consistency starts with one locked recipe per cultivar.

LED vs HPS: a redistribution, not a verdict

The cleanest head-to-head remains a 2018 Italian trial. Magagnini, Grassi and Kotiranta grew clones of the drug-type cultivar G-170 under three spectra at the same canopy intensity — about 450 µmol·m⁻²·s⁻¹ — on an 18-hour vegetative and 12-hour flowering schedule: high-pressure sodium (HPS) and two LED spectra, Valoya's AP673L and NS1. Then they repeated the entire experiment. HPS produced taller plants and more flower dry weight. Both LED treatments produced higher THC and CBD concentrations, and NS1 the highest CBG content. Total cannabinoid yield per plant did not differ significantly between the three light sources, and the pattern was similar in both runs.

Read carefully, that is evidence that spectrum redistributes output between flower mass and concentration — the two numbers a biomass buyer prices separately. A cultivation partner who swaps HPS for LED, or the reverse, between contract cycles can move the certificate of analysis and the kilogram count in opposite directions without changing genetics, feed or room. A 2021 Frontiers in Plant Science review of the spectrum literature adds the compound-specific caveat: visible LED light can augment THC and terpene accumulation, but not CBD. There is no general "quality" direction from a fixture change — only per-compound responses that have to be measured cultivar by cultivar.

Blue, green and far-red: three different levers

Blue:red ratio. Danziger and Bernstein's 2021 trial in Industrial Crops and Products — its findings summarized in the Frontiers review — varied the blue:red ratio across three medical cannabis cultivars. Blue-rich light stimulated accumulation of CBGA, the central precursor from which THCA, CBDA and CBCA are made, relative to far-red-rich HPS light; the downstream cannabinoids responded less strongly and in cultivar-specific ways, and blue light was the most effective lever for keeping plants compact. Namdar and colleagues' rich-blue flowering treatment likewise raised CBGA content and the CBGA:THCA ratio. The discipline point for product teams: a precursor shift is not a finished-potency guarantee. What reaches the certificate of analysis depends on how each cultivar converts CBGA onward.

Green light and the lower canopy. Hawley's 2018 subcanopy work, as reviewed in Frontiers in Plant Science, put light inside the canopy rather than above it. Blue+red and blue+green+red subcanopy treatments both raised Δ9-THC content and CBGA levels, and the green-containing treatment had the stronger effect on terpene accumulation in both the upper and the lower canopy. This matters because light intensity falls steeply with distance from the fixture — in the Guelph intensity work, small changes in distance from the source produced substantial differences in leaf-level light — so the bottom of a tall plant grows in a different light environment from the top. Spectrum decisions are usually made for the top of the canopy; the chemistry of the lower canopy is where within-batch variance is manufactured.

Far-red as a timed signal. Far-red light (roughly 700–750 nm) works through the phytochrome system: a falling red:far-red ratio is the plant's shade signal, triggering elongation. Peterswald and colleagues (Scientific Reports, 2025) tested whether end-of-day far-red could substitute for full-spectrum hours in three medicinal cultivars. Adding 2 hours of far-red in darkness after a 10-hour day raised total cannabinoid yield in Northern Lights by nearly 70% over the 12-hour control (0.43 vs 0.25 g per plant), far-red treatments elevated THC concentration in both high-THC varieties, and that schedule used 5.5% less power than 12 hours of full light. Dose discipline is the catch: 4 hours of far-red layered onto a full 12-hour photoperiod significantly reduced trimmed flower weight in all genotypes. Far-red is a signal applied at a specific time of day — not a background ingredient to blend into a spectrum and forget.

UV: strong claims, narrow evidence

The popular claim — add ultraviolet light, raise THC — has been tested in two modern controlled programs and failed in both. Llewellyn and colleagues (Frontiers in Plant Science, 2022) grew the high-THC cultivar Meridian at 600 µmol·m⁻²·s⁻¹ with either UVA added across flowering or UVA+UVB for the last 20 days. There were no UV effects on any biomass metric and no effects on inflorescence cannabinoid concentrations. The single positive signal sat in the sugar leaves, where UVA+UVB raised THC concentration by about 30% without changing the total THC held in that tissue — a shift inside a trim fraction, not a potency gain in flower. The authors' conclusion is blunt: no commercially relevant benefits of adding UV to indoor production. An earlier UVB study they discuss, using short-wave 287 nm LEDs, found only deleterious effects on morphology, physiology, yield and quality.

Huebner and colleagues (Frontiers in Plant Science, 2024) ran the most granular UV trial to date — three UV spectra at five intensities on the CBD cultivar Fedora 17 — and found the same null for cannabinoids: no UV treatment altered the cannabinoid profile. One treatment did move terpenes. The lowest-dose, UVA-dominant variant (99:1 UVA:UVB at 1.81 W/m²) raised linalool by 29%, limonene by 25% and myrcene by 22% versus the no-UV control, at constant yield and cannabinoid concentration. Higher UVB shares shrank leaf area, and the authors judged only that one low-dose variant suitable for practical use in commercial cultivation. On current evidence, UV is a narrow terpene tool with a dose ceiling — not a potency tool.

Intensity and DLI: photons buy grams

Intensity is the best-documented lever in this literature. Rodriguez-Morrison, Llewellyn and Zheng (Frontiers in Plant Science, 2021) grew cannabis across canopy PPFDs from 120 to 1,800 µmol·m⁻²·s⁻¹ through a 12-week flowering period: dry inflorescence yield increased linearly across the entire range, and apical inflorescence density and harvest index rose with it — more light meant more flower, and a larger share of the plant that was saleable flower. Cannabinoid potency showed no response to intensity; terpene potency shifted only slightly. Llewellyn's 2022 trial found the same shape over a commercial range in a high-THC cultivar: moving from 600 to 1,000 µmol·m⁻²·s⁻¹ — the 600 level corresponds to a daily light integral (DLI) of 25.9 mol·m⁻²·d⁻¹ under 12 hours — made inflorescence dry weight 1.6 times higher, other biomass metrics 1.3–1.5 times higher, and harvest index 7% higher, again with no change in cannabinoid concentration.

One hemp result cuts the other way, and it is worth keeping in view precisely because it breaks the pattern. In a 2025 Frontiers in Plant Science vertical-farm trial, raising white-LED intensity from 200 to 600 µmol·m⁻²·s⁻¹ during flowering raised total CBD in the hemp cultivar Queen Dream by about 37%, alongside coordinated upregulation of cannabinoid-pathway genes, including CBDAS. It was a small trial, in a different chemotype and production system from the drug-type studies — which is the point. Whether intensity moves potency is a per-cultivar question, not a law. What is consistent across every program is the input side: the crop is responding to total photons delivered, PPFD multiplied by hours (the DLI), so the commercial question is grams per unit of light delivered — and the energy and infrastructure that light requires — not maximum light for its own sake.

Photoperiod and the consistency problem

Photoperiod is usually treated as a fixed industry constant: 18 hours to vegetate, 12 to flower. Peterswald and colleagues (Plants, 2023) tested nine flowering schedules across three medicinal cultivars — static 10-, 12- and 14-hour days plus mid-flower switches of two and four hours. Treatments starting at 14 hours produced the highest flower biomass in all three lines. But in the two high-THC lines, a static 14-hour day significantly reduced THC concentration, while in the CBD line Cannatonic the 14-hour starts raised CBD concentration enough to lift total CBD yield by 50–100%. The authors conclude that the assumption that 12:12 is optimal for all lines is incorrect. For product teams, the same finding doubles as a consistency warning: one facility-wide schedule change is a yield improvement for one cultivar and a potency change for another.

That is the thread connecting every section of this brief. Where researchers held a light recipe constant and repeated it — Magagnini's duplicate runs are the clearest case — spectrum effects reproduced. Variance enters when the recipe moves: a fixture swap, a dimming or schedule change, a spectrum experiment rolled out across cultivars it was never tested on, or a canopy gradient nobody changed on purpose. A brand buying biomass sees the end of that chain on a certificate of analysis. Unexplained COA drift between batches is, very often, a lighting recipe that drifted first — in the grow room, weeks earlier, and auditable only if the recipe was written down.

What this means for product teams

  • Buy the recipe, not the fixture. Require cultivation partners to record, per cultivar, the spectrum class, canopy PPFD or DLI, and photoperiod — and to treat any change to those as a process change. A fixture swap, a new dimming profile or a schedule adjustment should trigger comparative certificates of analysis on the first batch under the new recipe before it ships against the old specification.
  • Price intensity as yield, never as potency. For drug-type cannabis, the controlled evidence is consistent: more photons buy grams and harvest index, linearly to very high PPFD, at flat cannabinoid concentration. A supplier pitch that ties a lighting upgrade to higher THC percentage needs that supplier's own per-cultivar trial data behind it.
  • Ask UV users for the waveband, the dose and their COAs. Added UV showed no cannabinoid benefit in two controlled programs, and the one positive result is low-dose UVA lifting specific terpenes (linalool, limonene, myrcene) at constant cannabinoids. If a growing partner runs UV, those three items are the diligence — a fixture name is not.
  • Pilot every spectrum or schedule change on one cultivar first. Blue-rich spectra, end-of-day far-red and longer photoperiods all produced genotype-dependent effects — in the photoperiod work, opposite in sign between THC and CBD lines. One room, one cultivar, with COAs, is the unit of evidence; rolling a recipe out on another cultivar's data is how COA drift gets built into a supply contract.
  • Audit the lower canopy. Light falls steeply below the top of the canopy, and subcanopy work shows chemistry differs by canopy layer. Ask how lower-canopy flower is lit, and whether it is segregated or blended at harvest. Blending policy is a consistency decision that belongs in the specification, not an afterthought at the trim table.
